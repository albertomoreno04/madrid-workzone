# MADTwin — Project Plan

**Working title:** *MADTwin: A Heterogeneous Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning Digital Twin for Robust Temporary Traffic Management around Urban Workzones in Madrid.*

**Deliverables:** peer-review paper, thesis chapter, and a working software prototype.

---

## 1. One-paragraph pitch

Madrid frequently runs concurrent urban roadworks across its arterial and ring-road network, and the current practice for temporary traffic management (TTM) is largely heuristic: fixed-time signal plans, manual lane assignments, and reactive VMS messages. MADTwin is a calibrated digital twin of a Madrid corridor whose closed-loop controller is a *heterogeneous multi-agent reinforcement learning* policy that simultaneously decides signal timings at affected intersections, lane re-allocations in the vicinity of the workzone, and dynamic VMS detour advisories. The policy is trained with domain randomization and adversarial demand perturbation so that it generalizes across unseen workzone configurations and demand regimes. A physics-informed spatiotemporal predictor produces short-horizon queue and spillback risk estimates that the controller consumes as exogenous features. Evaluation goes beyond average delay: we report throughput, spillback probability, emissions (HBEFA), and an equity index over Madrid census zones, and we run formal robustness stress tests. The artefact is an open Madrid-M30 SUMO scenario plus reference policies — a benchmark contribution in its own right.

## 2. What is already in the repository, and what we keep

The current repository is a scaffold: a Python 3.11 project with Ruff, mypy, and pytest set up; a SUMO routes file and an OSM extract of a Madrid corridor; and a baseline-analysis script that parses SUMO's `summary.xml`, `tripinfo.xml` and `statistics.xml` into a structured JSON of metrics (including a halting-count "spillback proxy"). Everything else — workzone modelling, predictive layer, control layer, real-data ingestion, validation harness, robustness testing, equity analysis — is missing. The scaffold itself is sound, so we keep the SUMO choice, the OSM corridor as a starting point, and the baseline-metrics extraction logic (which will be refactored into `src/madrid_twin/eval/baseline.py`). The corridor will very likely need to be expanded — a single short corridor is too small to give MARL interesting coordination problems, and a too-large network destroys training tractability. The right scope is the M-30 inner ring plus its main feeders or a comparable sub-network around a known recurrent workzone area, sized for roughly twenty to fifty controllable intersections.

## 3. Research questions

The plan is organized around four questions. **RQ1** asks whether a heterogeneous MARL controller — co-trained over signal, lane, and VMS agents — beats classical adaptive control (max-pressure, SCATS-like fixed-time/actuated baselines) and single-agent DRL on delay, throughput, spillback risk, and equity around workzones. **RQ2** asks how well the learned policy generalizes to unseen workzone configurations (location, duration, capacity-drop magnitude) and demand regimes (off-peak, peak, event-driven). **RQ3** asks how robust the controller is to distribution shift — demand surge, capacity-drop misestimation, sensor dropout — and whether adversarial / domain-randomized training measurably helps versus standard training. **RQ4** asks what the equity impact of optimal TTM is: whether disadvantaged neighborhoods absorb disproportionate detour delay, and whether adding an equity term to the reward materially changes operational outcomes. Each RQ maps to an experiment block in Phase 4.

## 4. Architecture

The system is organized as four layers, each of which is a clearly delimited package under `src/madrid_twin/`.

The **data and ground-truth layer** ingests Madrid open data — the Ayuntamiento traffic intensity feed for M-30 detectors, the workzone permits portal, EMT's GTFS-RT for buses, AEMET weather, and DGT historical data — plus OSM and INE census shapes. It is responsible for producing the calibrated demand inputs and the held-out ground truth used for validation. Data versioning runs through DVC; this is non-negotiable for a research artefact that you want others to reproduce.

The **calibrated simulation layer** is built on SUMO 1.20+ (microscopic with mesoscopic periphery where useful) and is responsible for the digital twin proper. It contains a workzone parametric module that takes a configuration (edges affected, lanes closed, start/end time, expected capacity drop) and modifies the network accordingly. Capacity drop follows the Highway Capacity Manual 2016 ranges with empirical Madrid corrections where available — and is itself treated as uncertain. Demand is calibrated against detector counts using W-SPSA (Tympakianaki et al., 2015), the de-facto standard for offline SUMO calibration, with a gravity-model prior. Route choice is dynamic stochastic user equilibrium with logit perception error; rerouting in response to VMS uses SUMO's `<rerouter>` mechanism driven by the control layer through TraCI. Validation uses the GEH statistic (target: GEH < 5 on at least 85% of detectors, the Wisconsin DOT industry standard) plus RMSE on observed corridor travel times.

The **predictive layer** consumes the simulator's state (and, where available, real detector feeds) and forecasts queue length, spillback probability, and link travel time at 5, 15 and 30-minute horizons. The baselines are the standard ST-GNN benchmarks: DCRNN, GraphWaveNet, AGCRN and STAEformer, implemented through PyTorch + `torch-geometric-temporal`. Our methodological contribution at this layer is a physics-informed variant that adds an LWR conservation residual to the training loss — link flow in minus flow out minus density change — turning the GNN into a soft enforcer of mass balance, with a hyperparameter trading off data fit against physical consistency. Uncertainty intervals come from split conformal prediction, which gives distribution-free coverage guarantees that the downstream controller can use as a confidence channel. This layer is intentionally decoupled from the controller so it can be evaluated against the public PEMS-BAY / METR-LA benchmarks as well.

The **optimization and control layer** is the headline contribution. The controller is a heterogeneous multi-agent reinforcement learning policy with three classes of agent: signal-control agents at each controllable intersection (action: phase / cycle / split adjustments), lane-manager agents on each candidate edge (action: discrete lane-assignment over a small set of physically feasible re-allocations including reversible lanes where applicable), and VMS / detour-advisor agents at each variable message sign (action: which detour message to display, parameterized over a small set of pre-validated detours). Observations are local — detector counts, queues, predicted travel-time on the relevant outflow links, and the conformal interval as an uncertainty feature — augmented with the workzone descriptor. The primary algorithm is MAPPO under the centralized-training-decentralized-execution paradigm, with parameter sharing within each agent class to keep sample complexity tractable; QMIX is run as an ablation. Robustness is achieved through two mechanisms operating jointly: domain randomization over capacity-drop magnitude, demand multiplier, sensor noise, and incident occurrence; and an RARL-style adversarial demand perturbation agent that learns to inject worst-case demand within a budget, forcing the protagonist policy to harden against it. The reward is a weighted combination of negative network delay, throughput, queue-spillback penalty (triggered when queue length exceeds a fraction of the upstream link length), an emissions term from HBEFA, and an equity penalty defined on the Gini coefficient of travel-time impact across census zones.

The **evaluation and experimental harness** runs scenarios, collects metrics, performs ablations and statistical tests, and produces publication-ready tables and plots. Baselines include fixed-time signals (current Madrid practice), max-pressure control (Varaiya, 2013), single-agent DRL on signals only, and a centralized MILP solved on a small instance for an upper-bound reference. Statistical comparisons use paired bootstrap confidence intervals and the Wilcoxon signed-rank test across scenario replicates. Sensitivity analysis on the simulator's structural parameters uses Sobol indices (SALib). All experiments are tracked in MLflow against a project-local SQLite store, and reproducibility is pinned at the language level — `.python-version` (3.11), bounded versions in `pyproject.toml`, and an explicit `SUMO_VERSION` constant — with platform-specific install instructions documented in the README rather than packaged into a container image.

## 5. Technology stack — peer-review-grade choices

For the simulator we commit to SUMO 1.20+ with TraCI / libsumo because it is the dominant open-source microsimulator in ITS research and its peer-review legitimacy is firmly established; for the OSM-to-network conversion we standardize on `netconvert` and `osmWebWizard.py`. Demand calibration uses an in-house W-SPSA implementation following Tympakianaki (2015); the alternative PC-SPSA variant is kept as a fallback for ill-conditioned cases. Route choice and rerouting are implemented through SUMO's `<rerouter>` driven from a custom TraCI controller written against the simulator's stable Python API. For predictive modelling we adopt PyTorch + `torch-geometric-temporal` for the ST-GNN baselines (this combination is the citation standard in the 2023–2025 ST-GNN literature), augmented with a custom physics-informed loss; uncertainty quantification uses split-conformal prediction implemented through MAPIE or a small custom module to retain control. For multi-agent RL we adopt the PettingZoo API on top of a custom Gym wrapper around SUMO (the existing `sumo-rl` package by Lopes Souza et al. is a reasonable starting point but is signal-control-only — we extend it for heterogeneous agents). Training is done in RLlib (which has first-class MAPPO and QMIX implementations and is well-supported in MARL papers); TorchRL is an alternative if we want tighter PyTorch integration. Adversarial robustness follows the RARL (Pinto et al., 2017) recipe adapted to MARL. Equity computation uses Gini and Atkinson indices on per-zone travel-time deltas, with zones from INE census shapefiles. Emissions are produced through SUMO's built-in HBEFA model. Experiment tracking is MLflow against a project-local SQLite store; data versioning is DVC. Reproducibility is encoded at the language and project level — `.python-version` pins Python 3.11, bounded versions in `pyproject.toml` pin the dependency surface, `madrid_twin.config.SUMO_VERSION` documents the SUMO pin, and a `Makefile` exposes one-shot reproducible targets — with platform-specific SUMO install instructions documented in the README. CI runs Ruff, mypy, pytest, plus a fast "smoke twin" scenario on every commit.

## 6. Novelty positioning relative to the current literature

The MARL traffic-control literature has a well-defined center of gravity around single-action signal control on synthetic grids — work by Wei et al. (CoLight, MPLight), Chu et al. (MA2C), and others. The novelty levers we exploit are four. First, **heterogeneous action types** (signals + lanes + VMS) are uncommon: most published MARL traffic controllers act on a single action class. Second, **urban workzone TTM** is the operating regime: most workzone-control literature focuses on motorway lane closures with MPC or rule-based logic, not coordinated urban response with rerouting. Third, **equity-aware rewards** are an emerging requirement (Cui, Levin and others, 2023–2025) and most MARL traffic papers omit them entirely. Fourth, **adversarial demand robustness** is borrowed from robust-RL but is not yet standard in MARL traffic control. Beyond these, a **calibrated open Madrid-M30 SUMO scenario** with documented OD calibration is itself a community contribution; reviewers respond well to artefacts that lower the cost of entry for follow-on work.

## 7. Phased roadmap

The realistic timeline for one focused researcher is six to eight months end-to-end; with a small team it compresses. **Phase 0 (one to two weeks)** restructures the repo into `src/madrid_twin/{sim, data, predict, control, eval}`, pins SUMO and Python versions at the language level (`.python-version`, `pyproject.toml`, `madrid_twin.config.SUMO_VERSION`), wires up DVC for data versioning, a project-local SQLite-backed MLflow store for experiment tracking, GitHub Actions CI, and a `Makefile` of pinned, one-shot reproducible targets. **Phase 1 (four to six weeks)** delivers the calibrated digital twin: open-data ingestion, expanded network, W-SPSA OD calibration, the workzone parametric module, and a validation report meeting GEH and travel-time RMSE targets. The output of Phase 1 is the *Madrid-M30 1.0* scenario — a deliverable in its own right. **Phase 2 (four to six weeks)** delivers the predictive layer: ST-GNN baselines, the physics-informed variant, and conformal interval calibration on held-out workzone scenarios, with comparison against public ITS benchmarks where applicable. **Phase 3 (six to ten weeks)** is the main contribution: implementing the heterogeneous-agent environment, MAPPO and QMIX training, parameter sharing, curriculum from no-workzone to severe-workzone, domain randomization, and RARL-style adversarial training. **Phase 4 (three to four weeks)** runs the evaluation programme — baselines, ablations, generalization, robustness stress tests, equity analysis, statistical significance, and sensitivity. **Phase 5 (four weeks)** is write-up and release: paper draft targeting *Transportation Research Part C*, *IEEE Transactions on Intelligent Transportation Systems*, or *ITSC*; thesis chapter expansion (more context, more ablations, more discussion); and the public release of code, scenario, and trained policies. Buffer of two to four weeks is realistic.

## 8. Evaluation programme

The evaluation programme is the part reviewers scrutinize hardest, so it is worth spelling out concretely. Primary metrics are average network delay per vehicle, total throughput, queue-spillback probability at workzone-adjacent links, p95 travel time on corridor OD pairs, CO₂ and NOₓ emissions (HBEFA), and a neighborhood-level Gini of travel-time impact. Baselines are (a) Madrid current-practice fixed-time signals with default VMS, (b) max-pressure control on signals only, (c) actuated SCATS-like control, (d) single-agent DRL on signals only with hand-coded lane and VMS rules, and (e) a centralized MILP solved on a small instance as an upper-bound reference. Ablations remove (i) the physics-informed loss in the predictor, (ii) the multi-agent coordination (independent learners only), (iii) the adversarial demand component, (iv) the equity term in the reward, and (v) the conformal uncertainty feature in observations. Generalization is measured on a held-out set of workzone configurations the policy never saw at training time. Robustness is measured under demand multiplier shifts (±20%, ±40%), capacity-drop misestimation (±30%), 10% sensor dropout, and a synthetic incident. Statistical comparisons use paired bootstrap and Wilcoxon signed-rank with at least thirty replicates per configuration. Sensitivity analysis uses Sobol total-order indices on the structural parameters of the simulator and reward weights.

## 9. Risks and mitigations

OD calibration with W-SPSA can fail to converge on sparse detector coverage; the mitigation is a strong gravity-model prior and a fallback to PC-SPSA. MARL training is notoriously unstable; the mitigation is curriculum learning from a no-workzone baseline, parameter sharing within agent classes, and conservative reward scaling. Real-data sparsity in the Madrid open feeds is likely; the mitigation is synthetic augmentation generated by the calibrated twin, combined with a request — already anticipated — for additional municipal or DGT data. Lane re-allocation actions must be physically feasible on the actual streets; this requires a manual labelling pass on candidate edges to define the discrete action set, and the safety constraint is enforced as a hard mask in the policy. Compute is non-trivial for MARL with adversarial training; we budget for a single GPU node and use sample-efficient algorithms (MAPPO, parameter sharing) plus vectorized simulation.

## 10. What to do first — concrete next actions

The immediate next steps, in order, are: restructure the repo into the four-package layout; pin Python via `.python-version` and SUMO via `madrid_twin.config.SUMO_VERSION` with platform-specific install notes in the README; identify the target network scope on a map (we recommend M-30 inner ring plus arterial feeders) and re-run `netconvert` on a larger OSM extract; set up a DVC remote and the project-local MLflow store (already wired through `madrid_twin.eval.tracking`); write a small "open-data probe" script that pings the Ayuntamiento traffic intensity endpoint and the workzone permits feed to confirm we can actually ingest both at the cadence we need; and finally draft the W-SPSA calibration loop against a single weekday peak hour. Each of these is a one-to-three-day task and unblocks the rest. None of this requires the predictive or MARL pieces yet, which keeps the early dependency chain shallow.
